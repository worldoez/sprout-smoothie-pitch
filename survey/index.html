The form

What we asked and how the form worked. 72 responses came in, 55 were kept after cleaning. 42 questions in 8 sections, run on Google Forms. Every question below keeps its real wording, type and answer options.

How the form works

From the first screen to the cleaned data set.

72 responses opened the form

Google Forms collected the answers. Nobody was paid; this was a convenience sample.

Q1 screening: how often are you on campus?

Answering “Rarely or never on campus” ends the form there and then.

Form ends: 4 people were not eligible.

Eight sections run in order

  1. Eligibility
  2. Drink habits and spend
  3. Importance of six factors
  4. Agreement statements
  5. Product choices and place
  6. Price and purchase intent
  7. Free text
  8. Demographics

Hidden attention check

Buried inside the agreement statements: one statement tells people to pick Disagree. It sits between ordinary statements and looks like one of them.

Price and intent

What people would pay for a drink, what they would pay for their own smoothie build, and how likely they would be to buy it.

Free text

Two open questions: why that price and intent, and anything else. Optional writing, coded later into themes.

Demographics

Gender, home region, and whether they live on campus. Last, so it cannot colour the earlier answers.

Prices people gave

Candidate prices and revenue

Share who would still buy at each candidate price. Hover, tap or focus a bar for the revenue index.

Revenue index is price multiplied by share of buyers; it ignores cost per cup.

What the analysis says

  • 77% would pay HK$25, 55% HK$30, 40% HK$35. The revenue-maximising price on this curve is HK$25.
  • Median HK$30 (bootstrap range HK$27-37); average HK$31.5. In their words: “Uniqueness, price” (respondent row 57, purchase intent 5/5) · “Mostly the price. I watch my spending, so I would only buy one if it costs about the same as a boba or a bottled drink. I would treat it as an occasional treat on hot days.” (respondent row 68, purchase intent 3/5)
  • HK$25 has the highest revenue index (price x share); give a range, not a point, and remember cost per cup is not included.
  • 4 of 15 pricing tests are statistically significant. The clearest is the paired test: the same people say they would pay far more for a smoothie than they usually spend. Differences between groups (past buyers, residents, gender, origin) are mostly not significant, which means we cannot tell, not that they are equal.
  • 92% would pay more than they spend now; a strong result, but hypothetical.
  • Usual spend goes with willingness to pay (p = 0.0008); group differences are mostly not detectable.
  • Usual spend varies by group; for study year, 1 of 3 tests are below 0.05 and the groups are small, so the evidence is mixed.
  • HK$25 vs HK$28 is a 17-point difference in buyers on stated demand; a real test needs about 117 people per price.
  • The best price depends on a cost we have not measured; get the real cost per cup before quoting a margin.

Would they buy

Stated purchase intent, the reasons counted from the open answers, and support for the pre-order app and loyalty scheme.

Purchase intent for the smoothie they built

Reasons counted from the open answers

Share of the coded answers that mention each theme. Hover, tap or focus a bar for the count and the 95% range.

Themes come from AI coding of the free text; two people still have to verify them.

What the analysis says

  • 70% say probably or definitely (95% range 56%-80%); stated intent overstates real buying.
  • The most-cited reasons are taste enjoyment, price value, convenience access; see the full table for what holds people back. In their words: “I would purchase a healthy fruit based drink often if it is convenient and well priced.” (respondent row 3, purchase intent 4/5) · “i don't have smoothies normally but i guess if it were with friends maybe id try?” (respondent row 22, purchase intent 3/5)
  • The R keyword pass ranks taste enjoyment, low need habit, price value, variety menu; the AI coding ranks taste enjoyment, price value, convenience access, low need habit. The AI coding reads the answers and is more accurate; neither is final until two people verify.
  • Pre-order app 64% agree vs loyalty programme 43%. In their words: “A mango or strawberry smoothie with yogurt would be great in summer. A student discount or loyalty card would help a lot.” (respondent row 47, purchase intent 4/5)
  • Affordability and health-first positioning both need rewording. In their words: “Fresh fruit and a fair price matter most. I already buy boba and coffee, so a fruity, refreshing drink for hot afternoons would be a nice change, as long as it is not too sugary or slow to get.” (respondent row 66, purchase intent 4/5)
  • Demand (70%), price headroom (HK$30 vs HK$10) and taste-first positioning support the product; the risks are the small convenience sample, stated (not real) behaviour and unmeasured cost.

Segments

Three small groups of students who value different things, and how much group difference the data can actually show.

The three segments

Bars show the share in each segment who would buy. Hover, tap or focus a bar for the size and prices.

What the analysis says

  • 3 small segments (segment 1 (12 people) = taste + health; segment 2 (19 people) = taste + freshness; segment 3 (22 people) = taste + price).
  • Very few differences are detectable. Green cells are the only real signals, and some of those are fragile across cleaning tiers. In their words: “I mainly go for cheap vending machine drinks, only about 1-2x per week, and will occasionally treat myself to a coffee at cafe330. I consider smoothies more as food than as a drink.” (respondent row 30, purchase intent 4/5)
  • Years 3-5 (n = 38) have a median WTP of HK$30 vs HK$21 for years 1-2 (n = 12). Years 1-2 vs 3-5: p = 0.046; trend (Spearman): p = 0.35; year groups (Kruskal): p = 0.12. 1 of 3 tests are below 0.05, the youngest group is small and many tests were run, so treat it as a lead, not a finding.

How sure are we

What survives different cleaning choices, and how many people a precise number would need.

Tests that change with cleaning

Four conclusions flip between the cleaning tiers. These are they.

    Tiers: A is all submissions, B is cleaned, C is cleaned with extreme prices blanked. Median WTP and the best price are the same in every tier.

    Sample size: what we have against what a precise share needs

    Target is plus or minus 8 points on a percentage, worst case. Recruit more than the target because some responses get cleaned out.

    What the analysis says

    • The median WTP is the same in every tier; only the averages move because of tier A's extreme answers.
    • 4 of 31 tests change with cleaning (mostly because of tier A's extreme answers); median WTP and the revenue-maximising price are the same in every tier.
    • We have 55; a precise percentage (+/-8 points) needs about 151.
    • 5 respondents who never buy drinks had spend answers blanked; the two Q9 scoring rules differ by only 1.7 points in taste share; no one chose a purchase-intent score below 2.
    • Comparisons that survive correction in the main sample: On campus daily (vs 1-4 d) vs Campus drinks / week; Price weighted > median vs Weight: freshness; none of them is about smoothie willingness to pay or purchase intent.

    Limits

    The sample is small: 55 respondents, mostly undergraduates, from one campus convenience sample. Prices and intent are what people say, not what they do; the open answers are AI-coded and not yet verified; cost per cup is not measured. 4 of 31 tests change with cleaning.
